# V79 Junior AI Academy — Team Project, Weekly Review & Project Skills Model

## Purpose

The Junior AI Academy should teach more than AI tools. Learners should leave knowing how to work with other people, plan work, take responsibility, handle disagreements, respond to feedback and finish a real project.

The program therefore uses **teams of three** throughout the course.

Each team operates as a small **AI Studio Team** and develops one long-running project from Week 1 through the final showcase.

---

# 1. Team structure

Each team has exactly three learners wherever class size permits.

At any point, the team has three clear responsibilities:

### Team Leader
Age-appropriate responsibilities:
- Helps the team agree on the weekly goal.
- Makes sure everyone has a task.
- Checks that nobody is left out.
- Keeps the team focused on the deadline.
- Gives the team's short weekly status report.
- Asks the instructor for help when the team is stuck.
- Does **not** boss the other learners around or do all the work.

### Creator / Builder
Age-appropriate responsibilities:
- Helps turn the team's ideas into the weekly artifact.
- Keeps project files, prompts, visuals or scripts organized.
- Makes sure the team actually produces something.

### Checker / Researcher
Age-appropriate responsibilities:
- Checks facts and sources when research is involved.
- Checks the V79 AI Creator Code and STOP privacy rule.
- Looks for missing pieces, mistakes and quality problems.
- Helps the team test work before submission.

All three children still create, discuss and make decisions. The roles are **responsibilities, not job boxes**.

## Leadership rotation

There should be **one accountable Team Leader at a time**, but the leadership role should rotate by project phase so every learner experiences leadership.

Recommended:
- **Weeks 1–5:** Leader A
- **Weeks 6–10:** Leader B
- **Weeks 11–16:** Leader C

For younger groups or learners who need more support, the instructor may shorten or adjust leadership periods.

The outgoing leader should perform a simple handover:
- What have we finished?
- What still needs work?
- What problems should the next leader know about?

This teaches leadership as responsibility and communication rather than status.

---

# 2. The long-running team project

The final product should **not** begin in Week 15.

The project begins in **Week 1**.

Every mission gives the team one new skill or project asset. By Week 16, the team has a finished product, evidence of its process and a presentation.

Examples:
- Saint Lucia visitor guide or campaign.
- Reef or sea-turtle awareness project.
- School event promotion package.
- Children's educational story/media product.
- Healthy snack brand concept.
- Hurricane-preparedness education campaign.
- Anti-litter or recycling campaign.
- Dream game concept and promotional package.
- Community problem-solving campaign.
- Mini media brand.

Projects should be age-appropriate, supervised and achievable with the available tools.

---

# 3. Weekly project cycle

Every week follows a simple project-management rhythm:

## PLAN
What are we trying to finish this week?

## DO
Who is doing what?

## CHECK
Is it correct, safe and good enough to submit?

## SUBMIT
Send the team's weekly task for instructor review.

## REVIEW
Read the instructor's feedback.

## IMPROVE
Fix what needs fixing and carry the improved work into the project.

This is essentially a child-friendly weekly sprint without requiring children to learn adult project-management jargon first.

Older learners can gradually be introduced to terms such as:
- goal
- task
- owner
- deadline
- milestone
- risk
- issue
- review
- revision

---

# 4. Weekly submission

At the end of every week, each team submits a **Weekly Studio Check-In**.

The submission should include:

1. **This week's deliverable**  
   The actual poster, prompt set, research card, image, storyboard, audio, video, presentation section, plan or other task.

2. **Team Leader Report**  
   Three short questions:
   - What did we plan?
   - What did we finish?
   - What do we need help with?

3. **Task Board**
   - To Do
   - Doing
   - Done

4. **Risk / Problem Check**
   - What could stop us?
   - Did anything go wrong?
   - What will we do about it?

5. **Individual Contribution Check**
   Each learner answers:
   - What did I help with?
   - What did I learn?
   - What will I do next week?

This makes individual accountability visible even though the major product belongs to the team.

---

# 5. Instructor review workflow

Every weekly submission should be reviewed by an instructor/team member.

Recommended states:

- **Draft**
- **Submitted**
- **Under Review**
- **Needs Changes**
- **Approved**

Instructor feedback should be brief and actionable.

A child-friendly feedback pattern:

### ⭐ Strong
What the team did well.

### 🔧 Improve
One or two things to fix.

### 🚀 Next
What the team should focus on next week.

Teams should be allowed to revise a weekly submission after feedback.

The purpose is not punishment. The purpose is to teach:
**submit → receive feedback → improve.**

---

# 6. Age-appropriate grading

Use a simple 4-part rubric.

## Ages 6–8
Use symbols/words rather than heavy scoring:
- 🌟 Great job
- 👍 On track
- 🔧 Needs a little work
- 🤝 Needs teacher help

Review four areas:
- **Learning** — Did the team understand the week's idea?
- **Creation** — Did they make the required thing?
- **Teamwork** — Did all three participate respectfully?
- **Responsibility** — Did they complete roles and respond to feedback?

## Ages 9–12
Use a 4-level rubric:
- 4 — Strong
- 3 — Ready
- 2 — Developing
- 1 — Needs support

Review:
- **Skill / Understanding**
- **Quality / Accuracy**
- **Teamwork / Communication**
- **Responsibility / Project Management**
- **Safety / Ethics** when relevant

A weekly numeric score may be stored for reporting, but feedback should matter more than points.

# 7. Basic project management

The goal is to teach habits, not turn children into certified project managers.

## Project Goal
“What are we making and who is it for?”

## Milestones
“What big pieces do we need to finish?”

## Tasks
“What small jobs must happen next?”

## Owner
“Who promised to do this task?”

## Deadline
“When does this need to be ready?”

## Status
“To Do / Doing / Done”

## Risk
“What might stop us?”

## Response
“What can we do if that happens?”

### Younger learners: The Uh-Oh Plan
For ages 6–8, call risk management:

## 🚧 Our Uh-Oh Plan

- What could go wrong?
- How can we stop it?
- Who can help us?

Example:

**Uh-oh:** Our recording is too noisy.  
**Plan:** Find a quieter place and test before the final recording.

### Older learners: Risk Card
For ages 9–12:

- Risk
- How likely?
- What could happen?
- Prevention
- Backup plan
- Owner

Keep ratings simple: Low / Medium / High.

---

# 8. Conflict management

Disagreement is treated as a normal part of teamwork.

The course should teach learners how to disagree **without attacking each other**.

Use the **CALM Team Method**:

## C — COOL DOWN
Stop arguing. Lower voices. Take a short pause if needed.

## A — ASK & LISTEN
Each person gets a turn:
“What do you think happened?”
No interrupting.

## L — LOOK FOR FAIR CHOICES
Find at least two possible solutions.

Examples:
- Combine both ideas.
- Test both ideas.
- Vote when the decision is low-stakes.
- Take turns.
- Split the task.
- Ask for instructor guidance.

## M — MAKE AN AGREEMENT
Choose what happens next and who will do what.

The leader helps the team follow CALM but is not the judge.

## When to get an adult immediately
CALM is **not** used to privately handle:
- bullying
- threats
- harassment
- unsafe behavior
- repeated exclusion
- discrimination
- sharing private information
- anything that makes a learner feel unsafe

Those go directly to the instructor.

---

# 9. Team Charter

In Week 1, each team creates a one-page **AI Studio Team Charter**.

It contains:

- Team name.
- Team members.
- Current Team Leader.
- Our project idea.
- How we will make decisions.
- How we will share work.
- How we will use CALM when we disagree.
- What respectful teamwork looks like.
- What we will do if someone cannot complete a task.
- Our promise: no one gets left out.

For ages 6–8, the charter can use pictures, checkboxes and instructor support.

---

# 10. Conflict reflection

If a meaningful disagreement occurs, the team can complete a short **Fix-It Card**:

- What happened?
- How did everyone feel?
- Which CALM step did we use?
- What did we agree?
- What can we do better next time?

This should not become a punishment form. It is a reflection tool.

---

# 11. Weekly project milestones

The 16-week course can build one continuous final product:

### Week 1 — Team & Project Launch
Team formation, charter, first leader, project idea, audience.

### Week 2 — Prompt Plan
Create reusable prompts for the project.

### Week 3 — Safety & Ethics Plan
Privacy, permission, honesty and responsible-use rules.

### Week 4 — Research Pack
Verified facts and sources.

### Week 5 — User / Audience Profile
Who is the project helping or speaking to?

### Week 6 — Visual Direction
Images, style, colours, concept art or visual identity.

### Week 7 — Story / Message
Core story, script, written content or narrative.

### Week 8 — Audio
Narration, podcast segment, sound or audio advertisement.

### Week 9 — Video
Storyboard and short video asset.

### Week 10 — Presentation
Explain the project clearly.

### Week 11 — Promotion
Poster, social-style content, campaign or promotional material.

### Week 12 — Workflow Plan
Map how all project pieces connect.

### Week 13 — Test & Improve
Get feedback from another team or test audience and revise.

### Week 14 — Value / Mini-Business Thinking
What value does the project create? Who might use it? How could it be sustained?

### Week 15 — Final Production
Complete missing pieces, quality check, risk review and rehearsal.

### Week 16 — Demo Day
Final product, presentation, team reflection and celebration.

---

# 12. Collaboration skills taught explicitly

Students should learn and practice:

- Taking turns.
- Listening before responding.
- Asking for another person's idea.
- Dividing work fairly.
- Making decisions.
- Giving useful feedback.
- Receiving feedback without becoming defensive.
- Saying “I disagree because…” respectfully.
- Asking for help.
- Keeping promises.
- Admitting when a task was not completed.
- Renegotiating a task before the deadline instead of hiding the problem.
- Giving teammates credit.
- Solving disagreements.
- Knowing when an adult must step in.

---

# 13. Accountability without blame

The Team Leader reports status, but the leader is **not responsible for another child's behavior**.

Accountability means:

> “I said I would do this. Did I do it? If not, did I tell my team early and help make a new plan?”

Every learner should be accountable for their own commitments.

The team is jointly accountable for the final product.

---

# 14. Final project evidence

At the final showcase, each team presents:

1. The finished product.
2. The problem / audience.
3. Important research.
4. How AI helped.
5. What humans changed or decided.
6. One risk they managed.
7. One disagreement or challenge they solved.
8. What each member contributed.
9. What they would improve with more time.

Each learner also completes an individual reflection so the course recognizes both team success and individual learning.

# 15. Platform requirements

The current V79 learner progress structure can store basic assignment submission text, but the Junior Academy model needs a more complete team workflow.

Recommended platform additions:

## Teams
- Team ID.
- Team name.
- Course ID.
- Exactly three learner IDs where possible.
- Current leader ID.
- Role history.
- Team charter.
- Long-running project record.

## Weekly submissions
- Team ID.
- Mission / assignment ID.
- Artifact links/uploads.
- Team leader status report.
- Task board snapshot.
- Risk/problem update.
- Individual contribution reflections.
- Submitted timestamp.

## Review / grading
- Status: Draft / Submitted / Under Review / Needs Changes / Approved.
- Instructor ID.
- Rubric ratings.
- Strength feedback.
- Improvement feedback.
- Next-step feedback.
- Review timestamp.
- Resubmission history.

## Project management
- Team tasks.
- Task owner.
- Due week/date.
- Status: To Do / Doing / Done.
- Risks.
- Risk level.
- Prevention / backup response.
- Risk owner.

## Collaboration
- Leadership history.
- Optional team check-in notes.
- Optional CALM / Fix-It reflection.
- Instructor intervention notes where appropriate.

These records should be visible to instructors without exposing sensitive notes to other students.

---

# 16. Certificate / completion recommendation

Course completion should require:

- Required lessons completed.
- Required weekly team submissions completed.
- Required individual reflections completed.
- Final team product submitted.
- Final presentation completed.
- Individual contribution confirmed.

A learner should not fail solely because another teammate struggled. Instructor review should separate **individual completion** from **team product quality** where necessary.
